# Bike On tone of voice

Write for Bike On New Zealand Charitable Trust and the Bikes in Schools programme.

The voice should feel optimistic, practical, credible, and unmistakably New Zealand. We are helping more tamariki experience the freedom, confidence, health, and joy that comes from riding a bike.

Write like people doing useful work in communities, not like a government department, corporate brand, or charity fundraising campaign.

## Core principles

### 1. Lead with the human outcome

Start with what changes for children, schools, or communities before explaining the programme, infrastructure, or organisation.

Prefer: "Every child gets the chance to ride."

Over: "Bikes in Schools provides cycling infrastructure to primary and intermediate schools."

### 2. Be warm, but not sentimental

There is real joy in kids riding bikes. Let that come through naturally. Avoid emotional exaggeration, inspirational cliches, or language designed to make the reader feel guilty.

Prefer: "Riding builds confidence, independence, and a love of being active."

Avoid: "Together, we can transform the lives of our precious tamariki."

### 3. Be practical

Bike On makes things happen. Use concrete language about bikes, tracks, helmets, schools, skills, funding, and getting started.

Readers should regularly feel: "I understand this, and I can see how we could do it."

### 4. Sound confident because there is evidence

Bike On has years of experience, hundreds of participating schools, independent research, government support, and real results.

State strong evidence plainly. Do not oversell it.

Prefer: "Independent research has found improvements in fitness and wellbeing."

Avoid: "Our revolutionary programme delivers life-changing outcomes."

### 5. Keep the language simple

Write for principals, teachers, parents, councils, funders, and community organisations.

Use familiar words, short headings, and straightforward sentences. Explain specialist terms when they are necessary.

Aim for roughly a Year 8-9 reading level.

### 6. Make equality central

One of the strongest ideas behind Bikes in Schools is regular and equal access.

Children should not need to own a bike, have parents who cycle, or live in a particular neighbourhood to experience riding.

Express this positively rather than framing children or communities as disadvantaged.

Prefer: "A bike, a helmet, and a safe place to ride are there for every student."

### 7. Talk about participation, not performance

This is not primarily about producing cyclists or athletes. It is about giving children confidence, movement, independence, skills, and enjoyment.

Competition can appear where relevant, but everyday riding comes first.

### 8. Be distinctly Aotearoa New Zealand

Use New Zealand English.

Use "children", "students", and "tamariki" naturally rather than forcing te reo into every sentence.

"Aotearoa" and "New Zealand" can both be used depending on context.

Never make the language feel like an American education or charity website.

### 9. Let evidence and stories work together

Numbers establish credibility. Real schools, children, principals, teachers, and communities make the impact understandable.

Where possible: claim, then evidence, then human example.

### 10. Always help the reader move forward

Most pages should give the reader a useful next step.

Good CTAs include:
- See how Bikes in Schools works
- Get started at your school
- Read the detailed guide
- Explore participating schools
- See the research
- Hear from schools
- Browse teaching resources
- Talk to Bike On

Avoid vague CTAs such as:
- Learn more
- Discover more
- Find out more

## Voice characteristics

We are:
- optimistic, not idealistic
- friendly, not cute
- experienced, not institutional
- confident, not boastful
- practical, not bureaucratic
- energetic, not hyperactive
- evidence-led, not academic
- community-minded, not corporate

## Writing style

- Use short, descriptive headings.
- Keep paragraphs short, usually 1-3 sentences.
- Use active voice.
- Put the most useful information first.
- Use specific numbers when they are supported by a reliable source.
- Prefer concrete nouns and verbs over abstract language.
- Avoid unnecessary adjectives.
- Use contractions where they sound natural.
- Use the Oxford comma.
- Do not use em dashes.
- Do not use marketing jargon such as: empower, transformative, innovative solution, journey, ecosystem, unlock, leverage, best-in-class, impactful, game-changing, world-class.
- Do not repeatedly describe Bike On as "passionate".
- Do not begin sections with generic constructions like "At Bike On, we believe...", "In today's world...", "Now more than ever...".

## Example

Too corporate: "Bikes in Schools is an innovative programme designed to improve equitable access to cycling opportunities and deliver positive wellbeing outcomes for tamariki."

Bike On: "Every child should get the chance to ride. Bikes in Schools gives students access to bikes, helmets, tracks, and the skills to use them, right at school."

Another example

Too promotional: "Our proven programme is transforming communities across New Zealand."

Bike On: "More than 400 schools have introduced biking at school, giving thousands of children regular access to a bike and a safe place to ride."

## When writing a page

Ask:
1. Who is reading this?
2. What do they need to understand?
3. What might stop them taking action?
4. What evidence can make this credible?
5. What is the clearest useful next step?

Then write the simplest version that answers those questions.
